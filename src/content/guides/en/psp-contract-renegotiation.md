## Start 60 days out, not the week the notice arrives

Renewal clauses typically require notice one to three months before the end date. If the contract end date is not in a calendar you actually look at, it will renew on last year's terms. Put every provider's end date and notice period on a renewal radar, and start the preparation when the 60-day window opens: that leaves time to pull the data, build the case and hold two rounds of conversation.

## Price a year of your own volume against the contract

The negotiation brief starts with what you actually paid: twelve months of settled volume, by card type, market and currency, priced under the current contract, and compared with what was billed. Three numbers fall out of this. The **contract gap**: anything billed above the agreed terms, with the payment references (this is owed, not negotiated). The **tier position**: how close your volume is to the next tier, and whether a tier was ever applied. The **effective markup**: what the provider actually earns on your traffic once interchange and scheme fees are stripped out, which is the number the negotiation is really about.

## Build asks the provider can check, not wishes

Each ask should rest on a fact from your own data rather than on a market rumour. Typical asks, in order of how easy they are to win: correct the billing gap and credit it; apply the tier the volume already qualifies for; move from blended to interchange++ pricing so network costs are passed at cost; lower the markup on the card types and markets that carry most of your volume; remove or cap per-event fees that have grown with your business (refunds, retries, disputes); and align FX terms with your actual settlement currencies.

Keep two documents. An internal brief that includes the market references and the full reasoning, and a provider-facing fact sheet that states only what the contract and your data already say. Published list prices are your leverage; they are not something to hand the other side.

## Know what you will concede, and what you will not

A provider will ask for volume commitments, longer terms or exclusivity in exchange for rate. Decide beforehand which of those you can give. A volume commitment on a market you are growing is cheap; exclusivity that prevents a second acquirer is not. Model the scenarios: what the year costs at the current terms, at your target terms, and at the provider's likely counter. Bring the downside case as well as the target, because a forecast you cannot defend is a forecast nobody signs off.

## Compare like for like before you compare prices

A competing quote is only useful if both providers are priced on the same traffic and the same definition of cost. Headline rates differ in what they include: one blends scheme fees, another passes them through; one charges per authorisation attempt, another per settled payment; FX terms differ in the reference rate and the margin. Take a representative month of your own settled payments, price it under each proposal in full (interchange, scheme fees, markup, FX, per-event fees, and the expected cost of declines and disputes), and compare the totals. The cheaper headline rate loses that comparison more often than providers would like.

## After the signature

The renegotiated terms only pay if they are applied. Load the new contract, run the first three invoices against it, and check the tier and the markup landed as agreed. Then record the outcome: what was asked, what was won, and what it earned in euros over the following quarters. That record is what makes the next renegotiation faster.
