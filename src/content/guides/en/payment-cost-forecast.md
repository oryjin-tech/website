## Why a flat percentage fails

A blended rate applied to a revenue forecast assumes the mix, the rates and the per-event costs stay where they are. None of them does. Scheme fee schedules revise on published cycles, sometimes with only weeks of notice. Volume tiers reset or are crossed. A market launch changes the interchange category of a share of your traffic. Refunds, retries and disputes grow faster than volume. Each one moves the rate without touching revenue, and the budget variance shows up at the close with no explanation attached.

## The three inputs a payment cost forecast needs

**Volume trajectory**, by processor and by month, from your own history. With fifteen months of data, each month can be projected from the same month a year earlier at the year-over-year trend, which captures seasonality; with less, a run rate is the honest fallback. **Mix trajectory**: the share of commercial cards, cross-border and each payment method, because mix moves the rate more than any negotiated change. **Contract mechanics**: tier thresholds and when the projected volume crosses them, announced scheme fee revisions with their effective dates, FX terms, and contract renewal dates that may change the terms mid-year.

## A range, with named drivers, beats a point

A single forecast line invites the wrong conversation. A range (a conservative headline with a P10 to P90 band) makes the uncertainty visible and lets the finance team decide how much buffer to hold. Each projection should name its drivers: volume up 6%, commercial card share up 1.2 points, a scheme fee revision effective in October, a tier reached in November. When a driver is uncertain, say so rather than hiding it inside the number.

Guard the forecast against its own inputs. A seasonal spike should not be allowed to inflate the trend; clamp the growth rate. An approaching tier should be listed as a driver, not silently applied. The band is the P10 to P90 of the effective rate applied to projected volume, never the clamp read as an uncertainty.

## Scenarios finance can actually ask

The questions a forecast has to answer are conditional: if volume grows 30% next quarter, do we cross a tier and what does the fee line become? If we open Germany and Spain, what is the incremental cost and at what basket size does local acquiring pay for itself? If commercial card share keeps drifting at the current rate, what does it cost over four quarters? If the renegotiation lands at the target rate rather than the current one, what is the annualised saving, and the downside case? If the announced scheme fee revisions apply in full to our mix, what is the exposure? Each scenario is priced on your own data, never on a slider.

## Hold every closed month against the forecast

A forecast nobody checks is a guess with a spreadsheet. Snapshot the projection at the start of each month, then hold the closed month against it and attribute the miss to volume, mix and unit cost by processor. Over a few quarters this produces the one thing a CFO wants from the payment line: a budget that can be defended, because its accuracy is on record.
